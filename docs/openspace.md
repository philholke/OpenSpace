# OpenSpace — Product Vision

**OpenSpace turns the drawings of a space into a space you can shape, stand inside and furnish.**

The unit is yours. What you have is a set of drawings. Bring them in, get a digitised version of the space in 2D and 3D, block out the layout with rooms and furniture, collect the things that go in it, and see what it adds up to. One place for the whole planning phase, from the drawings you were handed to a scheme you can act on.

---

## The problem

The lease is signed and the clock is running. What you have is a PDF from the landlord, a technical drawing from the previous tenant, maybe a survey. They are accurate and completely inert. They tell you where the walls, the columns and the services are. They tell you nothing about what you are going to put in there.

The planning phase is where that gets decided: where everything goes, whether it works, what it will feel like, what you need to buy and what it will cost. Today that work is scattered across a printed plan with pencil marks, a Pinterest board, a folder of screenshots, a WhatsApp thread, a spreadsheet, and — eventually — a designer's drawings. Nothing is connected to anything else. Move a section and the spreadsheet does not know. Find a chair you love and the plan does not know. The layout, the look and the money are three separate conversations about the same room.

So decisions get made on the basis of whichever fragment is in front of you, and the questions that span the fragments — *what happens to the budget if I add ten covers, does the sofa I pinned actually fit where I want it* — are the ones nobody can answer quickly.

## The product

**Bring the drawings in.** Upload the floorplans, lease drawings or technical drawings you already have. They become a digitised, true-to-scale model of the space — boundary, walls, openings, columns, services — available immediately as both a 2D top-down plan and a navigable 3D view. Real dimensions, not an image.

**Shape it.** Lay out rooms and sections, then place furniture and equipment inside them. Everything is a shape you can add, move, resize, rotate and delete. The primitives are deliberately simple, because the work is arrangement, not modelling. As the plan changes, the numbers that follow from it change with it — covers, area per cover, back-of-house share, circulation width.

**Stand inside it.** The same space at eye level, navigable. It does two jobs. It shows you what you have planned in a form a top-down drawing structurally cannot: how tight the aisle between banquettes really is, what you see from the entrance, where the dead corner is. And it lets you judge how the room feels, which is half of what you are actually deciding and the half a plan always hides.

**Collect what goes in it.** A separate album for inspiration and intent. Paste in Pinterest pins and references, or go straight to a product page and pin that. Attach one or more shopping links to any reference, so a thing you like becomes a thing you could buy, and the album becomes a working wish list rather than a mood collage.

**Cost it.** A running estimate built from what is actually in the plan — the furniture, the equipment, the fittings — priced from the items you have attached. Not a quantity survey, but a real-time answer to *what is this costing me so far*, that moves when the layout moves.

**Dress the surfaces.** Floor and wall finishes applied to rooms and sections, visible in the plan and in the 3D view, and carried through as an area-based line in the costing. A material choice becomes a visual decision and a budget decision in the same move.

## How it holds together

The plan is the source of truth, and a thing you place on it is the same thing you pinned and the same thing you priced. That single link is what makes this one product rather than three tools sharing a login.

It also means specificity can arrive late. Early on you drop a plain 1600×800 box called *sofa*, because right now you only need to know whether a sofa of that size fits. Later you find the actual one, attach it, and the box inherits a picture, a price, a link and its real dimensions — which may well push back on the layout you drew. You are never blocked on a decision you have not made yet, and nothing you decide has to be re-entered somewhere else.

## Who it is for

- **F&B and hospitality operators** planning the fit-out of a unit they have taken, working out the layout and the shopping list before or alongside engaging a designer.
- **Owner-operators** doing the planning themselves, who were never going to commission a full design package.
- **Designers and consultants** who want a client's intent — layout, references, budget — expressed in one place rather than reconstructed from a conversation.
- **Project and fit-out managers** who need the layout, the item list and the cost to stay in agreement as things change.

## Principles

**Speed over precision.** This is a planning instrument, not a construction document. Being roughly right this afternoon beats being exactly right in a fortnight.

**Always editable, never a black box.** Everything in the space is a shape a person can grab, move or overrule. Anything the system produces — including the digitised drawing itself — is a starting point you correct, not an answer you accept.

**Honest about scale.** Every number shown traces back to a real dimension, and where the scale is uncertain the product says so. A confident wrong cover count is worse than no cover count.

**Feel is a first-class output.** How many fit and how it feels are equally load-bearing, and most tools only answer the first.

**Nothing entered twice.** Layout, references and cost are views of the same set of decisions. Making a decision in one of them updates the others.

## Where it goes

- **Schemes side by side.** Develop three layouts for the same unit and compare them on covers, flow, feel and cost rather than by memory.
- **Start from a concept.** Name a brand or a concept and get a proposed starting point — the operating parameters it implies and a first layout built to them. Somewhere to argue with, which is faster than somewhere to begin.
- **Shareable by link.** Send a scheme to a partner, a landlord, a designer or a contractor without exporting anything, and collect comments on the plan itself.
- **A handoff pack.** Leave with the plan, the numbers, the references, the item list and the reasoning, in a form a designer or a contractor can pick up.
- **Concept kits.** A brand's own standards — seating, spacing, service model, palette, signature pieces — reused across every new site, so the tenth outlet starts where the ninth finished.
- **From wish list to order list.** The item list carried through to what has been ordered, what has arrived and what it actually cost against the estimate.
- **The economics attached.** Covers, area per cover and turnover assumptions carried through to what the space could plausibly earn, so a layout decision reads as a business decision.

## What it is not

Not a CAD tool and not a route to construction documents. Not photoreal visualisation. Not a procurement platform. Not a replacement for a designer — the intent is to make the time you spend with one far more productive, not to remove them.

## Success

Someone with a set of drawings and an empty unit can, in an afternoon, reach a layout they believe in, stand inside it, know what goes in it and roughly what that costs — and walk into the next conversation with a scheme rather than a question.
